# Lesson: How Old Houses and Mishandled Food Affect Health and Learning

Draft for review. Every number has a source, listed at the end. Most studies show a link, not proof of cause; students should say which is which. The lesson goes step by step, and each level goes deeper.

## Level 1: Notice the signs

In the house:
- Paint that peels, chips or turns to dust, in a house built before 1978.
- A musty smell, water stains, or black or green spots on walls.
- Mouse droppings, chewed holes, or roaches, especially in the attic, walls or heating vents.
- Old plaster walls that are cracked or being drilled or sanded.

In the food:
- Frozen food that is soft, or has big ice crystals or frozen-together clumps. That can mean it thawed and was frozen again.
- Cold food sold warm, or a store freezer that is not cold.

## Level 2: Know the facts

Lead paint in homes (HUD, CDC, EPA):

| Fact | Number |
|---|---|
| US homes with lead paint | 34.6 million (29% of all homes) |
| Homes with badly worn lead paint | 18.2 million (15%) |
| Homes with lead dust hazards | 21.9 million (19%) |
| Share of lead-paint homes built before 1978 | 89% |
| Safe blood lead level for a child | None. CDC says no level is safe |
| What lead does | Lowers IQ, attention and school results; the damage cannot be undone |

Damp, mold and pests (Berkeley Lab and EPA studies, NIH studies):

| Fact | Number |
|---|---|
| Higher risk of breathing and asthma problems in damp, moldy buildings | 30% to 50% higher |
| US asthma cases linked to home dampness and mold | about 21% (4.6 million people) |
| Yearly cost of that asthma | about $3.5 billion |
| Inner-city homes with mouse allergen, in one NIH study | 95% |
| Mouse allergen and asthma, Baltimore children | linked to worse asthma, more strongly than roaches |

Asthma and school (CDC):

| Fact | Number |
|---|---|
| School days missed for asthma in one year (2013) | 13.8 million |
| Extra school days missed by a child with asthma | about 2.3 per year |

Food safety (USDA):

| Rule | Number |
|---|---|
| Danger zone, where germs grow fast | 40°F to 140°F |
| Longest time perishable food may sit out | 2 hours (1 hour above 90°F) |
| Refreezing | Safe only if thawed in a refrigerator; never if it sat out more than 2 hours |
| Food thawed and refrozen | Loses quality, because water leaks out when it thaws |

Hunger and learning (research reviews):

| Finding | Source |
|---|---|
| Food insecurity, even mild, is linked to behaviour, emotional and school problems from infancy to the teen years | 2017 review of 23 studies |
| Hunger as a baby or toddler predicts lower thinking and social skills in kindergarten; more episodes, worse results | Early Childhood Longitudinal Study |

## Level 3: Measure it yourself

- Lead: a lead dust test of the floors and window sills; a blood lead test for young children, from a doctor or the health department.
- Damp: a moisture meter; look and smell after rain.
- Pests: count droppings in the same spot each week. Clean them wet, never by sweeping: CDC says to spray with disinfectant first, wear gloves, and air the room out.
- Asbestos: plaster or ceilings from before about 1980 should be tested before drilling or sanding.
- Food: a thermometer in the fridge (40°F or below) and freezer (0°F). Check frozen food for ice crystals and clumps.

## Level 4: Follow the chain

Students trace each link, and say whether a study shows a link or a cause:

House or food problem → health problem → missed school or lower attention → lower learning results.

Example: damp, moldy home → asthma → about 2.3 extra missed school days a year → less learning time.
Example: chipping lead paint → lead dust → higher blood lead → lower IQ and attention, for life.

The truck question: the safe time food can be out of its cold is a fixed rule (2 hours, or 1 hour above 90°F). How long a real shipment was actually out cannot be guessed. It has to come from the truck's or store's temperature records. Students should ask for those records, not estimate them.

## Level 5: Act before the damage is done

- Every one of these problems can be fixed: lead paint can be removed or sealed, leaks fixed, mold cleaned, pests sealed out, and food kept cold.
- Landlords and sellers of homes built before 1978 must tell buyers and renters about known lead paint (federal lead disclosure rule).
- Call the local health department or housing code office to report hazards and ask for inspections.
- Get young children tested for lead.
- Keep a record: dates, photos, test results. Records are what the law and the students can judge.
